Proof of concept - estimated data

Separate flood zone 2 and 3 climate change layers

Flood map for planning shows climate change as one extent covering flood zones 2 and 3 together. This proof of concept explores how "flood zone 2 with climate change" and "flood zone 3 with climate change" could be shown separately, in the menu, on the map and in the key.

These are wireframes, not the live map. The map is a shared component maintained in a separate repository, so it can't be changed from this prototype. The menu, buttons and key use the live component's own stylesheet and markup so they look like the current service, and they respond when you change a layer.

Behind them is a screenshot of the live map. Only one climate change extent exists today, so flood zone 2 and flood zone 3 with climate change are estimated. They are not flood modelling and must not be used for decisions.

To use a different location, see how the map is made.

How the map is made

The map image is a screenshot of the live service, so the basemap, flood zone 2, flood zone 3 and the climate change extent are the real data. The service only has one climate change extent, so the split into flood zone 2 and flood zone 3 with climate change is estimated:

  1. Flood zone 2 with climate change is the whole climate change extent (present day zones 2 and 3 plus the extra area climate change adds).
  2. Flood zone 3 with climate change is the part of that extent nearest to present day flood zone 3.
  3. It is sized so that zone 3's share of the extent matches zone 3's share of present day flood zone 2.

This is a design estimate. It ignores ground levels, flood depths and defences, so the real extents will differ.

What the example shows

The example location is a river floodplain near Down Ampney Road. Climate change adds only about 6% to the present day flood zone area, mostly as strips a few pixels wide along the edge and a few small patches. Present day flood zone 3 is about 76% of the present day flood zone 2 area. Splitting those strips in two leaves very small areas of each colour, so the styling has to work for slivers. This is why the extents use solid colours and not patterns.

Changing the location

  1. In the live service, choose Datasets: Flood zones and Climate change: 2070 to 2125. Leave Layer opacity at the default and switch off Map features.
  2. Take a screenshot of the map only, with no panels or buttons on top. Use the default outdoor style.
  3. Save it as climate-change.png in app/views/design-spec/fz-climate-change-layers-v3-10-0-0/images/source/.
  4. Optional: take the same view with Datasets: None and save it as base.png. This gives an exact basemap under the flood layers.
  5. Run python3 scripts/build-fz-climate-estimate.py (needs numpy, scipy and pillow). The wireframes show a tall map, so a wide screenshot is cropped. Use --focus-x and --focus-y (0 to 1) to choose the centre of the crop. The script prints how much of the extent it estimated as flood zone 3.

Current design

Reviewed from the map code (client/map/plugins/datasets/floodZones.js, colours.js and terms.js in DEFRA/fmp-map) and the 3.9.0.0 info panel.

  • Menu: Datasets (flood zones, surface water or none), then Climate change (present day or 2070 to 2125), then Map features and Layer opacity.
  • Present day: flood zone 2 and flood zone 3 layers.
  • Climate change: a single "Climate change (2070 to 2125)" layer covering zones 2 and 3 together, shown with present day flood zone 2 and 3. Areas without data are shown with a dotted pattern.
  • Key: in climate change mode lists "Flood zone 2 (present day)", "Flood zone 3 (present day)", "Climate change (2070 to 2125)" and "Climate change data unavailable".
  • Help: the info panel text "How to use flood zones plus climate change" describes the "combined extent of flood zones 2 and 3".

Proposed map styling

Each of the four extents is a solid colour, with no patterns. In the example, the climate change areas are strips a few pixels wide and small patches, and splitting them in two makes them smaller. A hatch or dot pattern needs room to repeat, so at this size it reads as noise. Solid colours stay legible on slivers, match how the service styles layers today, and are the simplest to build.

Zone 3 with climate change is a deeper tone of the same warm family as zone 2 with climate change, in the same way that zone 3 is a deeper blue than zone 2 today. Users only need to learn one rule: darker means zone 3.

Draw order and overlap

  1. Flood zone 2 with climate change (bottom)
  2. Flood zone 2 present day
  3. Flood zone 3 with climate change
  4. Flood zone 3 present day (top)
  • Zone 3 with climate change sits above present day zone 2, so land that moves from zone 2 to zone 3 shows in the zone 3 climate change colour. This is the most useful change to see. If present day zones were drawn on top of it, most of the zone 3 climate change area in the example would be hidden.
  • Present day flood zone 3 stays on top, so it is never covered.
  • Zone 2 with climate change is the bottom layer, so it shows as the extra area beyond today's flood zones, as the single climate change layer does today.
  • Layers are not outlined. The extents are too small for an outline to help, and it would add weight to the thin strips.
  • The "data unavailable" dotted area is drawn at the top and is shown whenever either climate change layer is on.
  • Check how the service composites layers when it is built. The wireframes show a layer on top hiding the one beneath, with climate change layers at 75% opacity.

Colour is not the only cue

Solid colours make this harder, so the spec relies on several smaller cues together:

  • Lightness. Zone 3 with climate change is clearly darker than zone 2 with climate change (2.8:1), and zone 2 with climate change is much lighter than both blues.
  • Position. Zone 3 with climate change sits beside present day zone 3 and zone 2 with climate change sits further out, so layout supports the colour.
  • Map query. Selecting a point opens the info panel, which names the layer in words (for example "Flood zone 3 with climate change"). This works for people who cannot tell the colours apart.
  • Every key item has a text label, and the key only lists layers that are switched on.
  • The menu lets users switch the two climate change layers on one at a time, so they can see each on its own.

Risks to test: zone 3 with climate change (red) against present day zone 2 (mid-blue) has almost no difference in lightness (1.1:1) and relies on the hue difference. Check this with a colour blindness simulator and with users, on the real map and not only the swatches.

Location boundary and flood defences

Two existing map features are warm-coloured lines that sit on top of the flood zones. Both are close in colour to the climate change extents. Tick "Show a location boundary" above the wireframes to see the boundary with each option.

Location boundary {{ swatch('boundary') }} 2px red outline #D4351D, no fill Salmon and red are different enough 1.1:1. Almost the same red
Flood defence {{ swatch('defence') }} Solid orange line #F47738 1.7:1. Salmon is a pale version of the same orange 1.3:1 as shown on the map. A red-orange area next to an orange line

What this means on the map:

  • Boundary. The estimated zone 3 climate change shows as thin bands around zone 3, which are about the same width and colour as the boundary line, so they can be mistaken for boundary segments. In the key, the red outline for "Location boundary" would sit near a solid red square.
  • Flood defences. Defences often run along the edge of a flood zone, as in the live example, which is exactly where the climate change bands appear. An orange line beside a salmon or red-orange band relies on line against area to tell them apart.
  • Reports. The summary report and Product 1 map also draw the boundary in red, so the clash carries into those.
  • The live boundary has no fill or dimming, so the colours inside the boundary are the same as outside.

Options to decide between:

  1. Give both lines a white casing. It separates each from the flood colours beside it. But it makes the two lines look more alike, because both are a thin coloured line with a white edge, and the only difference left is red against orange.
  2. Make the two lines differ in more than colour, so they can be told apart without relying on colour. Two styles to compare are below. A tick-marked defence was tried and ruled out.
  3. Change the zone 3 with climate change colour. This only helps if the new colour is clear of both red and orange, and every warm colour is close to one of the two lines. So changing the colour alone isn't a good fix.
  4. Keep the colours and rely on line against area. It costs nothing, so test it first with users to see whether it is a real problem on typical maps.

Ways to make the two lines differ

  • B: casing on one side of the defence only. The flood defence gets a casing on one side only, and the boundary gets a casing on both sides and is heavier (3px). It is quiet. But the one-sided casing is subtle at small sizes, and the same side has to be kept all along the line.
  • C: different casing colours. The defence keeps a white casing on both sides and the boundary gets a near-black casing. The two differ by the colour of the edge as well as the line, and the boundary reads as a frame around the area.

You can compare these in the wireframes. Use the "Map style" and "Line style" options above them, or open a wireframe on its own with ?boundary=1&defence=1&lines=onesided&mode=dark (lines can be current, onesided or darkcase, and mode can be outdoor or dark). The flood defence lines are illustrative, not real data. They are straight-segment vector lines like the live ones. One follows the outer edge of the flood area, which is the worst case for the clash with the salmon band, and one crosses the zone 3 with climate change bands so you can see the orange line over the red.

Dark map style

Today the dark style draws the boundary in white and the flood defence in the same orange. Flood zone 2 is green and flood zone 3 is a pale green-white. The single climate change layer is dark red (#BF3D4A). The basemap in the dark wireframes is an approximation (the outdoor basemap inverted), not the real dark tiles.

Effect on the line styles:

  • Casing colour flips. A white casing disappears against the pale zone 3 and against the white boundary, so in dark mode the casing in B is near-black, and in C the colours swap: white casing on the boundary's partner line is not needed, so the boundary gets the near-black casing and the defence a white one is kept as in outdoor.
  • B works well in dark mode: a near-black edge on the orange defence stands out on the pale zone 3 and on green, and the white boundary with a black edge on both sides can't be mistaken for the orange line.
  • C keeps the near-black casing on the boundary, which is the least visible choice against a dark basemap, and the defence's white casing is the same as the white boundary. The two lines look alike on the pale zone 3, so C is weaker in dark mode.

Red in the dark style

Keeping red for climate change in the dark style is a problem for colour blind users. Simulating red-green colour vision deficiency (protanopia and deuteranopia) on the dark style shows:

  • The dark red climate change colour against the green flood zone 2 drops to a colour difference (CIE ΔE) of about 6, which is hard to tell apart (about 10 or less).
  • Red against the dark basemap is weak too, around 2:1 in luminance contrast. Red and black are close for many colour blind users.
  • Moving to a lighter pink doesn't fix it: pink against the green still depends on a colour vision that red-green colour blind users don't have.

So the wireframes use purples for climate change in the dark style, which red-green colour blindness leaves mostly unchanged:

  • Zone 2 with climate change: magenta-purple #C75BC0.
  • Zone 3 with climate change: deep purple #6A1B9A, darker, in the same way zone 3 with climate change is the deeper colour in the outdoor style.

A first try used a pale pink for zone 3 with climate change, following the pale present day zone 3. That was too close to present day zone 3 (luminance contrast 1.4:1), and the two were hard to tell apart on the map. The deep purple against present day zone 3 is 5.4:1 and a colour difference of 53 in the simulation, and the pair stays distinct for all three types of colour blindness. The weakest pairs are zone 2 with climate change against zone 3 with climate change (colour difference 22) and zone 3 with climate change against the dark basemap (22), which is lower because deep purple is close to the dark background. Blue and gold was worse (the defence nearly vanishes against gold). These are screen simulations, so please check them with a colour blindness simulator on the real map and with users.

The outdoor style has the same risk to a lesser degree: red against the mid-blue is mainly a hue difference. That is already noted in "Colour is not the only cue".

I'd take B forward for testing in both styles. No option has been chosen yet, so the wireframes show the current line styling until you choose.

Open questions for the data and dev teams

  1. Is a split estimated from the present day zones good enough to show stakeholders, or should the data team supply a proper zone 3 climate change extent first?
  2. Does separate flood zone 2 and flood zone 3 climate change data exist? Today the vector tile service (Flood_Zones_2_and_3_Rivers_and_Sea_CCP1) has one "Flood Zones plus climate change" layer.
  3. If not, what would it take to produce and publish them, and when could they be available?
  4. Do both extents use the same allowances (river peak flow central 2080s, sea level upper end to 2125)? Which probability does each use?
  5. Is the zone 3 climate change extent always inside the zone 2 climate change extent? Do the polygons overlap, or are they clipped so that zone 2 excludes zone 3?
  6. Could a climate change extent ever be smaller than its present day extent, for example where defences are ignored?
  7. Does "data unavailable" apply to both zones in the same places?
  8. What does the map query ("Get info") return for each layer? Today it returns one value for climate change, so the info panel, results pages and any reports would need new values.
  9. What is the impact on tile size, load time and the layer opacity slider of two climate change layers instead of one?
  10. Can the map plugin style two climate change layers in all three map styles? Today's layers take a solid fill and stroke colour from the style settings, so solid colours should work without new code.
  11. Does the downloadable Product 1 map need to follow the new styling?
